# Curriculum Workflow Principles

These principles are intended to guide administrators, departments, colleges, submitters, and reviewers at all levels. They emphasize transparency, consistency, trust, and shared expectations: people should be able to understand what a proposal needs, who reviews it, what each review stage is responsible for, and why a proposal is returned when revision is needed.

They also provide a larger frame for recommendations developed through Faculty Senate Academic Council working-group conversations about curriculum review, governance, and workflow. The callouts below show how those recommendations are represented in this framework.

---

## 1. Make Expectations Visible and Consistent

A curriculum workflow works best when expectations are visible before a proposal enters formal review. Submitters should know what information is needed. Reviewers should know what belongs to their stage. Later review bodies should be able to trust earlier work. Similar proposals should be handled in similar ways.

This does not mean every proposal needs the same preparation. It means the process should make differences clear: form expectations, workflow tiers, consultation needs, review responsibilities, and return reasons should be documented well enough that people are not guessing.

Clear documentation also helps the system improve. When expectations are explicit, recurring confusion can be traced to a form prompt, guidance gap, training need, or workflow design issue.

## 2. Implement Workflow Tiers

Curriculum proposals vary in consequence. A course description update should not require the same amount of proposal preparation or receive the same level of scrutiny as a program revision.

The proposed process embraces **workflow tiers** not as rigid categories but as practical guides for preparing the proposal, identifying needed consultation, and setting expectations for review depth. Tiers help everyone spend time most effectively.

{% include typography/callout.html class="warning" title="Key point" text="The level of preparation and review should match the scope of the change." %}

## 3. Define Clear Review Stages and Responsibilities

Most proposals move through similar formal stages: department, technical review, college review, Faculty Senate review, and final administrative review. This principle is about **division of responsibility**: what each stage is expected to review in accordance with the proposal tier.

**Later reviewers need to be able to trust earlier review steps, and should not routinely redo earlier work.** If a Senate committee is hunting for credit hour statements, it has less capacity to do Senate-level review across the whole curriculum.

A clearer division of responsibility would allow:

- Departments review academic coherence and local feasibility.
- Technical review checks form readiness and catalog/workflow issues.
- Colleges review programmatic fit, advising, resources, and intra-college coordination.
- Senate bodies review university-wide academic and governance questions.
- The Provost’s Office reviews institutional commitments and final alignment.

Return practices are part of that responsibility. If a reviewer sends a proposal back, the return should identify what needs to change, why it matters, and whether the issue belongs to the current stage or should have been addressed earlier. Recurring returns should be treated as evidence that the system may need clearer prompts, better examples, training, form changes, or a different consultation point.

## 4. Use Related Reviews Without Overreaching

Curriculum review should connect to related academic processes without becoming a substitute for them. This is a balance: proposals should draw on program review, assessment, accreditation, and planning where relevant, but curriculum workflow should not become a general-purpose review of teaching quality, instructional design, or syllabus language.

### Connect to existing reviews

Program review, assessment, accreditation, advisory boards, and other formal reviews should have operational consequence. When a proposal responds to those processes, the proposal is stronger and easier to evaluate.

For program-level changes, reviewers should expect to see whether the proposal is informed by:

- Academic Program Review
- Student learning outcomes assessment
- Accreditation or licensure review
- External or advisory-board feedback
- Student demand or field developments

### Keep curriculum review within scope

Proposal review should focus on broader curricular elements like learning outcomes, course goals, credit hours, grading mode, prerequisites, program requirements, student impact, resource implications, and institutional fit.

**There is no reason to require a syllabus for curriculum review.** This does not devalue course review. It allows curricular review to focus on curricular implications, not syllabus language.

Course review that addresses instructional design, syllabus language, grading scales, and related questions deserves a different kind of review through a teaching and learning body or similar process.

{% include typography/callout.html class="warning" title="Key point" text="Curriculum review should be informed by related academic reviews, but it should not become course-quality policing." %}

{% capture review_boundary_recommendations %}
- **Connect proposals to assessment, accreditation, and planning.** APR, assessment, accreditation, and strategic planning should inform the rationale where relevant.

- **Keep course-quality review in the right venue.** Instructional-design and course-materials review should be supported through appropriate teaching and learning processes, while curriculum workflow should rely on structured curricular fields that reviewers are positioned to evaluate.

## 5. Improve Kuali Form Prompts

Form fields shape what submitters think reviewers need. If a prompt is vague, defensive, or too narrow, proposals are more likely to provide generic answers or miss the real issue.

Kuali fields should help submitters focus on the questions reviewers actually need answered. The goal is not longer responses. The goal is clearer prompts that produce useful, consistent information.

Examples:

- A justification prompt should ask what prompted the change, what evidence supports it, and what outcome is expected.
- A budget prompt should ask about sustainability, not only new money.
- A duplication prompt should ask about collaboration, overlap, and curricular design, not simply whether a duplicate exists.

Well-framed fields reduce avoidable returns and make review more substantive.

{% include typography/callout.html class="warning" title="Key point" text="Kuali prompts should focus submitters on the questions reviewers actually need answered." %}

Draft starting point: [Kuali Form Guide]({{ site.baseurl }}/curriculum/kuali-form-guide.html), practical guidance for framing proposals so reviewers can understand what is changing, why it matters, and what downstream effects may follow.

{% capture field_framing_recommendations %}
- **Clarify the justification field.** Proposals should explain what prompted the change, what evidence supports it, and what outcomes are expected.
- **Reframe budget as sustainability.** Resource questions should include teaching load, course rotation, advising, staffing, infrastructure, enrollment, and long-term feasibility.
- **Redesign duplication and collaboration prompts.** Overlap should be treated as a design and collaboration question rather than a defensive compliance check.
- **Make consultation more visible.** Proposals should show how faculty consultation occurred, what concerns were raised, and how the proposal fits departmental priorities and staffing plans.
- **Simplify and validate form fields.** Information should be entered once, updated consistently, and not duplicated across multiple fields or related forms.
